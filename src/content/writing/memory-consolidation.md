You will forget most of today by next week. A few things will survive for years. The difference is a process called consolidation, and it happens long after the moment itself.

## Short-term is chemistry

A new memory starts as a change in how strongly neurons talk to each other. Within minutes, existing proteins at the synapse are modified and the connection gets stronger.[^1] This is fast and cheap, but it is also fragile: interrupt it and the memory is gone.

## Long-term is construction

For a memory to last, the neuron has to build. Repeated or emotionally important signals switch on genes that produce new proteins, and those proteins grow new synaptic connections.[^2] Block protein synthesis in that window and short-term memory still works, but nothing makes it to the long term.

> Short-term memory changes the strength of a connection. Long-term memory changes the wiring.

## Sleep does the filing

The hippocampus records the day quickly. During deep sleep it replays those patterns, and each replay nudges the cortex to store a more permanent, more general version.[^3] This is one reason sleep after learning matters as much as the learning itself.

## Why I care

Every design choice in agent memory, what to keep, what to merge, what to let fade, has a version the brain already solved. I'm reading the biology to steal the good ideas.

[^1]: This strengthening is called long-term potentiation, first described in the hippocampus in the 1970s.
[^2]: Much of this was worked out in the sea slug *Aplysia*, work that earned Eric Kandel a share of the 2000 Nobel Prize.
[^3]: "Systems consolidation": the gradual shift of a memory's dependence from the hippocampus to the neocortex.
